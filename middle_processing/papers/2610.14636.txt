 Abstract 

 Terminal rewards make multi-turn agents trainable without step-level annotations, but group-relative advantages provide little guidance about which intermediate decisions to adjust.
Failed trajectories also contain reasonable actions, while unfavorable decision patterns can recur as training proceeds.
We introduce PEARL (Process-Experience Aware Reinforcement Learning), which improves local credit assignment without an additional critic.
Given two continuations from the same history with different outcomes, PEARL locates their first divergence and builds a feature representation of each branch’s local decision.
It clusters these decision features in recent failure and success memories to identify recurring local patterns.
Using this pattern memory, PEARL selectively strengthens negative advantages at lower-reward decisions, while leaving positive advantages and the base policy optimizer unchanged.
On AppWorld, PEARL improves over ARPO on Normal and Challenge tasks at both 4B and 8B scales.
On ALFWorld, it achieves 97.9% mean success at 4B, 2.7 points above the strongest baseline, while remaining competitive at 8B.
Ablations of shaping location, success-bank filtering, and memory window support the value of selective, recurring-pattern-based updates.

 1 Introduction

 Multi-turn language agents can be trained from verifiable task outcomes without annotating every intermediate action.
The resulting reward, however, says little about which decisions along a long interaction deserve adjustment.
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replaces a learned critic with group-relative outcome advantages, but tokens in the same trajectory share the same reward-based comparison ( Shao et al., 2024 ) .
Actor–critic methods such as PPO can estimate state-dependent values, but require training an additional critic and do not by themselves provide labels for locally correct or incorrect actions ( Schulman et al., 2017 ) .
The first challenge is therefore to use step-level information from successful and failed trajectories to improve credit assignment without adding a critic or changing the underlying policy optimizer.

 The second challenge is to decide which local experiences are worth reusing.
Similar decisions may recur in lower-reward trajectories, but a failed trajectory also contains reasonable actions that should not receive extra penalties.
Moreover, the policy changes during training, so patterns observed early may become less relevant to its current behavior.
Repeated failure-side occurrence alone is thus insufficient: useful supervision must identify recurring outcome-associated decisions while accounting for successful behavior and the changing policy.

 Branching rollouts provide a starting point for both challenges by generating alternative continuations from a shared interaction history ( Dong et al., 2026 ; Ji et al., 2026 ) .
When their outcomes differ, the branches expose a local decision contrast without requiring a learned value estimate.
Yet one contrast does not prove that the differing action caused the outcome; the question is how to reuse such contrasts across trajectories without penalizing behaviors also seen in successful continuations.

 Figure 1: Overview of PEARL. Unequal-outcome branches provide local decision contrasts. Recurring decision patterns are stored in recent failure and success memories, success-side evidence filters ambiguous failure patterns, and retained failure patterns guide stronger negative updates.

 We introduce PEARL (Process-Experience Aware Reinforcement Learning), illustrated in Figure 1 .
For branches with different outcomes, PEARL localizes their first actual divergence and represents each selected decision with layer-wise token logits.
It clusters these features across updates in recent failure and success banks, so recurring patterns can inform later training decisions rather than being used only once.
Success-side matches exclude ambiguous failure clusters, while a sliding window limits the influence of older experience.
Only negative advantages at decisions matching retained failure-side patterns are strengthened; positive advantages and the base policy optimizer remain unchanged.

 The retained patterns are candidates for targeted updates, not labels of intrinsically incorrect actions.
Their value comes from combining a local outcome contrast with recurrence and a check against successful behavior.

 We evaluate PEARL on AppWorld ( Trivedi et al., 2024 ) and ALFWorld ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ) at both 4B and 8B model scales.
On AppWorld, PEARL improves over ARPO on both Normal and Challenge tasks at both scales.
On ALFWorld, PEARL achieves the best reported mean at 4B and remains competitive at 8B.
Controlled ablations compare pattern-guided with random shaping and measure the effect of success-bank filtering, while process diagnostics track how often the resulting memory activates shaping.

 Our contributions are:

 • 

 We turn terminal-reward differences between continuations from a shared history into local training signals, improving credit assignment without step-level annotations or an additional critic.

 • 

 We develop a selective memory for recurring decision patterns that uses successful experience and recent observations to focus extra negative updates, rather than applying them to every action in failed trajectories.

 • 

 We show that PEARL improves over ARPO on AppWorld and ALFWorld at both 4B and 8B scales; controlled ablations further examine the roles of targeted shaping and success-bank filtering.

 2 Related Work

 Group-relative and branching agent RL. 

 GRPO replaces a learned critic with within-group reward normalization and has become a common foundation for reasoning RL.
Agent-oriented extensions exploit additional rollout structure: GiGPO combines within- and across-group comparisons ( Feng et al., 2025 ) ; Tree-GRPO estimates intra-tree and inter-tree advantages from shared-prefix rollouts; and ARPO allocates branches using uncertainty changes along an interaction.
Sequence-level normalization and strategic trajectory abstractions provide related ways to restructure the optimization signal ( Zheng et al., 2025 ; Xue et al., 2026 ) .
These methods determine which trajectories to sample or how their current rewards become advantages.
In contrast, PEARL uses the resulting branches to build a cross-update memory of recurrent decisions and remains an add-on to the underlying branching optimizer.

 Credit assignment from sparse outcome rewards. 

 Long-horizon agent training must infer useful local updates from trajectory-level outcomes.
Recent approaches improve this inference through future-sensitive objectives, selective eligibility traces, information gain, entropy balancing, or learned process abstractions ( Ma et al., 2026 ; Mou et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2025 ; Dong et al., 2025 ) .
Hindsight Policy Optimization (HPO) derives step-level advantages without a critic by comparing current and success-conditioned hindsight distributions in a semantic intent space via Wasserstein distance ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) .
These methods construct finer action-level signals; PEARL addresses the complementary question of which locally contrasted decisions recur on lower-reward branches without corresponding success-side support.
It compares continuations from a shared branch point, aggregates matching decisions across updates, and strengthens negative advantages only after recurrence and success-disambiguation tests.

 Memory-based reward shaping and internal representations. 

 The closest representation-level precursor is MEDS, which clusters layer-wise target-token logits and uses their recurrence for dynamic reward shaping ( Liu et al., 2026 ) .
PEARL retains the useful idea that a token’s logit trajectory across layers is a compact signature of a decision, but changes both supervision and memory semantics.
Specifically, outcome-side labels come from discordant parent–branch pairs rather than whole-trajectory outcomes; regions start at the first true divergence; failure and success representations occupy separate banks; and success acts as an asymmetric filter before negative-only shaping. These labels identify candidate update locations, not ground-truth local errors.
HDBSCAN ( McInnes et al., 2017 ) permits an unknown number of patterns and leaves low-density observations as noise, while a sliding window limits the lifetime of representations collected under older policies.
Thus, PEARL is a training-time memory for selective credit assignment rather than an inference-time store of demonstrations or reflections.

 3 Method

 3.1 Overview

 For a prompt x x , an agent policy π θ \pi_{\theta} generates a multi-turn trajectory
 τ = ( a 1 , o 1 , … , a T , o T ) \tau=(a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{T},o_{T}) and receives a verifiable terminal reward R ⁡ ( τ ) R(\tau) .
Our base optimizer is ARPO, which samples multiple trajectories for the same prompt and may branch from an intermediate state to explore alternative continuations.

 PEARL augments ARPO with a cross-update memory that turns recurring lower-reward-side decision patterns into additional training signals.
As illustrated in Figure 1 , the method has four stages.
First, branches with different outcomes provide local failure–success comparisons.
Second, each divergent decision is represented by a compact decision-aligned feature.
Third, recent failure and success features are clustered separately, and success-side evidence is used to filter ambiguous failure patterns.
Finally, negative advantages are strengthened only for decisions matching the retained failure patterns.

 3.2 Local supervision from discordant branches

 A terminal failure reward does not reveal which local decisions should receive stronger negative updates.
Branches from the same saved state provide a more focused comparison because they share the same preceding interaction history.

 We therefore use only parent–branch pairs whose terminal rewards differ.

 Pairs with equal outcomes contribute no memory entries.

 4 Experiments

 We evaluate PEARL on AppWorld and ALFWorld, followed by controlled ablations and process diagnostics.

 4.1 Experimental setup

 Benchmarks and models. 

 We evaluate Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) on two multi-turn agent benchmarks.
AppWorld evaluates interactive coding agents on Normal and more compositional Challenge tasks over simulated applications, while ALFWorld evaluates text agents on Seen and Unseen embodied household tasks.
Success rate is the primary metric for both benchmarks.
The agent prompts for both environments are provided in Appendix C .

 Baselines. 

 We compare with GRPO, GiGPO, Tree-GRPO, and ARPO under the same model and environment infrastructure.
PEARL is built on ARPO because its branching rollouts provide the matched local comparisons required by Section 3.2 .
GRPO, GiGPO, ARPO, and PEARL use 16 rollouts per prompt; Tree-GRPO follows its tree-expansion setting and produces 24 physical leaves per prompt group.

 Training and evaluation protocol. 

 We evaluate AppWorld after 400 optimizer updates and ALFWorld after 300 updates.
All main experiments use three training seeds, and we report the mean and standard deviation across seeds.
The default PEARL configuration uses MCS10/MS5, W = 25 W=25 , K = 10 K=10 , T s = 1 T_{s}=1 , hard success-bank filtering, and failure-only advantage shaping.
Unless otherwise specified, ablations are conducted on AppWorld with Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 using a single controlled run and evaluated at step 400.

 4.3 Ablations

 We next examine the main design choices of PEARL, including shaping direction, shaping location, success-bank filtering, memory window, and clustering features.

 Effect of shaping direction. 

 Negative and positive shaping are not interchangeable.
Figure 4 (a) varies the shaping direction without cross-bank filtering.
Failure-only shaping achieves 52.58%/27.98% on Normal/Challenge, compared with 44.64%/25.42% for success-only shaping and 47.02%/24.78% for bidirectional shaping.
Adding success shaping to failure shaping lowers performance by 5.56/3.20 points.
Thus, among these shaping directions, strengthening negative updates on recurrent lower-reward-side patterns is more effective than adding positive weight to success-side patterns.
Section 4.4 examines the corresponding shaping activity and response length.

 Success evidence helps disambiguate failure patterns. 

 Patterns observed on failed branches may also occur in successful behavior and are therefore poor targets for additional negative shaping.
To isolate the success bank’s filtering role, we compare failure-only shaping with and without the cross-bank veto while keeping both banks and the clustering settings unchanged.
As shown in Figure 4 (b), enabling this filter improves Normal/Challenge performance by 5.16/3.04 points over no filtering.
This result supports using successful experience to reject ambiguous failure patterns before applying extra negative weight.
Section 4.4.1 examines a concrete example of this filtering.

 Pattern-guided shaping outperforms matched random locations. 

 We test whether the location of extra negative weight matters, rather than just its presence.
The control retains the same discordant-branch labels, banks, clustering, and success-bank filter as PEARL: a retained failure-cluster match still triggers shaping and determines its multiplier.
For each match, PEARL amplifies the negative-advantage tokens between the first divergent token and the end of that action.
The random-location control counts these tokens, then uniformly samples the same number of negative-advantage action tokens from anywhere in the same lower-reward trajectory and applies the same multiplier.
For example, if a matched action contains four eligible tokens, both variants amplify four tokens with that match’s multiplier; only the control chooses their positions at random.
Thus, the comparison changes where each matched shaping event is applied, not its token budget or weight.
At step 400, pattern-guided shaping achieves 57.74%/31.02% on Normal/Challenge, compared with 49.17%/25.37% for random shaping.
With each match’s token budget and multiplier controlled, the 8.57/5.65-point gains show that PEARL identifies more effective locations for negative updates than random selection within the same trajectory.

 Recent memory outperforms unlimited history. 

 The bank must supply patterns relevant to the current policy, so we test whether retaining only recent entries is more effective than keeping the entire training history.
With hard filtering and otherwise identical settings, extending the window from 25 updates to unlimited history ( W = ∞ W=\infty ) reduces Normal performance from 57.74% to 50.00% and Challenge performance from 31.02% to 26.38%.
Thus, retaining more historical experience does not necessarily provide a better shaping signal.
Section 4.4 examines how the memory window affects shaping activity during training.

 Ablation on clustering features. 

 We test which feature representation is most effective for modeling local decisions in PEARL.
We compare four HDBSCAN inputs while keeping all other settings fixed: late-half target logits (18 dimensions), the final normalized hidden state after the first divergent token has been processed (2,560 dimensions), mean-pooled final hidden states over the divergent action suffix (2,560 dimensions), and all-layer target logits (36 dimensions).
The first-token hidden-state baseline thus uses the post-token state, not the shared pre-token state used to predict that token.
All variants use MCS10/MS5 and Euclidean distance on L2-normalized features.

 As shown in Figure 4 (c), late-half target logits achieve 57.74% on Normal and 31.02% on Challenge.
They give the best Normal result while remaining within 1.43 points of the best Challenge result.
Compared with first-token hidden states, late-half logits improve Normal/Challenge by 10.72/6.24 points, and compared with all-layer logits by 7.94/4.00 points.
Mean-pooled hidden states obtain the highest Challenge score, but their Normal performance drops to 52.78%.
Among these complete representation choices, late-half target logits provide the strongest balance across the two splits at step 400 and are therefore used as the default clustering feature.

 4.4 Process diagnostics: how does PEARL use its memory?

 The ablations above establish how PEARL’s design choices affect final performance.
We next examine how memory retention and filtering translate into shaping activity, and why the resulting update is negative-only.

 Finite history preserves more usable failure signals. 

 Figure 5 shows that W = 25 W=25 activates failure shaping in 88 of 400 updates, compared with only 3 under unlimited history.
Unlimited history keeps features from earlier policies in both banks.
At update 400, one retained failure cluster contains 1,200 of 1,368 failure entries, consistent with accumulated entries obscuring smaller recent patterns in the density structure; at updates 100 and 300, success-bank filtering leaves no retained failure cluster (Appendix Table 4 ).
Even a large retained cluster shapes an update only when a current decision matches it.
These snapshots show why bank size alone is misleading: a dominant cluster or a cross-bank veto can leave few patterns that match current decisions.

 Figure 5: Failure-shaping activity with a 25-update window and unlimited history ( W = ∞ W=\infty ) on AppWorld (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, hard filtering, MCS10/MS5; one run per setting). Left: number of updates with active failure shaping in each consecutive block of 10 updates. Right: cumulative number of updates with active failure shaping, reaching 88 for W = 25 W=25 and 3 for unlimited history.

 Positive shaping reinforces an already rewarded signal. 

 The base objective already favors higher-reward continuations; success shaping adds weight to recurring decisions within them, whereas failure shaping strengthens penalties on recurring lower-reward decisions.
In the no-filter ablation, the positive signal is far from inactive: success-only shaping activates on 65% of the final 100 updates, compared with 40% for failure-only shaping, yet achieves lower task success.
When success shaping is added to failure shaping, mean evaluation response length rises from 4,145 to 7,770 tokens on Normal and from 4,367 to 8,505 on Challenge, while success declines.
The extra positive weight therefore changes generated behavior in the combined run without improving task completion, motivating PEARL’s focus on recurrent lower-reward decisions.

These cases expose a failure of outcome-only memory: an action can recur in failed trajectories while also supporting successful ones. By vetoing such shared patterns before shaping, PEARL avoids turning terminal failure into an extra penalty on reasonable preparatory behavior.

 5 Conclusion

 We introduced PEARL to turn sparse outcome feedback into selective local supervision for multi-turn agents without training an additional critic.
Rather than assigning extra blame to every decision in a failed trajectory, PEARL stores recurring failure-side patterns, uses successful experience to veto patterns shared across outcomes, and strengthens negative updates only where a retained pattern matches.
Across AppWorld and ALFWorld, experiments at two model scales show improvements over the underlying optimizer, while ablations and bank diagnostics support the roles of targeted shaping, success disambiguation, and recent memory.
These findings suggest that outcome-level feedback becomes more informative when past decisions are selected for reuse, without requiring a locally verified error label for every action.

 Appendix A Additional Experimental Details

 A.1 Default training configuration

 We train PEARL with Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (4B) and Qwen3-8B (8B) for 300 updates on ALFWorld and 400 updates on AppWorld. Each update uses eight task prompts and 16 trajectories per prompt, for a global batch of 128 trajectories. ARPO starts with eight trajectories per prompt and allocates the remaining rollout budget through branching, with one branch per trigger.

 Rollouts allow up to 50 agent turns on ALFWorld and 40 on AppWorld, with a response limit of 4,096 tokens per turn. The context limit for the accumulated interaction is 65,536 tokens for 4B models and 32,768 tokens for 8B models in both environments. Training uses sampling temperature 1.0 and top- p = 1.0 p=1.0 , without top- k k truncation.

 All four configurations use Adam with a constant learning rate of 10 − 6 10^{-6} , weight decay 0.1, and ( β 1 , β 2 ) = ( 0.9 , 0.98 ) (\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.98) . They use GRPO advantages, symmetric PPO clipping at 0.2, and an entropy coefficient of zero. The branching entropy probe uses 20 tokens, top- k = 128 k=128 , α = 0.5 \alpha=0.5 , β = 0.2 \beta=0.2 , and threshold 0.5.

 Evaluation uses a 4,096-token response limit and three greedy samples per task (top- k = 1 k=1 ). The evaluation curves in Figure 3 include checkpoints at 25-update intervals. ALFWorld uses the Seen and Unseen validation splits, and AppWorld uses the Normal and Challenge test splits. ALFWorld assigns reward 1 for task success and disables the additional invalid-action penalty. The environment prompts and action formats are given in Appendix C .

 The default PEARL banks use HDBSCAN minimum cluster size 10, minimum samples 5, a 25-update window, and a reclustering interval of 10 updates.
Failure shaping is enabled, success shaping is disabled, the shaping temperature is 1.0, and hard success-overlap filtering is used.
The code-level shaping-strength argument is an enable/disable gate in the current implementation; the exponential multiplier itself follows Equation 6 .
All four configurations use counterfactual bank labels derived from unequal-outcome parent–branch pairs. The 0.3 failure/success selection ratios retained in the ALFWorld launchers are legacy settings for the alternative turn-selection path; counterfactual labeling uses the paired regions directly.
