 Abstract
 Terminal agents have shown strong abilities in complex coding tasks. However, they often struggle to recover from errors during long-running execution (e.g., command errors, faulty implementations), eventually leading to task failures.
To this end, we present ReTerm, a self-improving terminal agent trained by learning how to recover from its own failed terminal tasks.
To support its training, we construct a customized data pipeline and produce ReTerm-10K, a high-quality dataset that provides effective recovery demonstrations for SFT cold start.
Furthermore, we propose ReTerm-GRPO, an online RL algorithm that forks from the agent’s own failure states to generate multiple independent self-recovery attempts. By learning from whether these attempts successfully complete the task, the agent self-improves through its own recovery experience.
Built on Qwen3.5-9B, our ReTerm-9B achieves 34.8% Pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2.0, improving over the base model by 14.6 points.
Further analysis shows that our method achieves consistently higher recovery rates across diverse error categories compared with standard GRPO training.
All code, data and model are released at https://github.com/twothsnd/ReTerm .

 1 Introduction
 The ability to interact with terminal environments has become increasingly important for agents to tackle complex coding tasks ( OpenAI, 2025 ; Anthropic, 2025 ) . It allows large language models (LLMs) to work within runtime environments and make progress on the tasks through long-horizon interactions. Recent works have constructed large collections of executable tasks to strengthen this capability. These tasks support supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on generated trajectories ( Yang et al., 2026a ; Jain et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Shi et al., 2026 ) and reinforcement learning (RL) with feedback from task verifiers ( Cao et al., 2025 ; Gandhi et al., 2026 ; Ivison et al., 2026 ; Shen et al., 2026 ) . Such efforts have achieved substantial performance gains on challenging benchmarks such as Terminal-Bench 2.0 ( Merrill et al., 2026 ) , highlighting the effectiveness of these training approaches.
 Despite these advances, terminal agents still encounter errors frequently during extended execution and struggle to recover ( Merrill et al., 2026 ; Feng et al., 2026b ) . For instance, an agent may meet a command error, misdiagnose its root cause, and continue acting in the wrong direction. To address this, a contemporary work, TermiGen ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) , actively injects hand-crafted errors into trajectories to generate recovery samples for training. However, these artificially constructed errors may exhibit a distributional mismatch with agents’ own naturally generated errors. Moreover, it relies solely on SFT, leaving the potential of RL to further enhance terminal agents’ recovery capability unexplored.
 To this end, we introduce ReTerm , a self-improving terminal agent trained to recover from failures produced during its own execution. We first construct ReTerm-10K , a recovery trajectory dataset generated by our dedicated data pipeline. Specifically, we use the base model to execute terminal tasks and collect failed trajectories together with the environment states they leave behind. Starting from these failure states, a stronger model is asked to perform recovery based on the inherited execution history. We then apply a series of quality filters to remove unsuccessful, malformed, non-compliant, or inefficient recovery trajectories, retaining only high-quality samples for SFT cold start. Unlike conventional trajectory synthesis that teaches an agent to solve tasks from a clean environment, ReTerm-10K explicitly teaches how to continue from a faulty environment state, identify what went wrong, and repair the remaining problems.
 Figure 1: An inference example revealing the difference in recovery behavior between models trained by GRPO and our proposed ReTerm-GRPO. With ReTerm-GRPO, the model formulates a stronger recovery strategy. 
 Furthermore, we propose ReTerm-GRPO , an online reinforcement learning algorithm that explicitly learns self-recovery from errors produced by the evolving policy. For each sampled failed rollout, ReTerm-GRPO preserves the resulting execution state and branches into multiple independent self-recovery attempts from the same failure state. Then, their verifier outcomes provide group-relative learning signals for optimizing recovery behaviors, turning self-generated failures into opportunities for self-improvement.
To support recovery training when a single training batch yields too few failed rollouts, we introduce a recovery task queue to store recovery tasks across training steps. Newly constructed tasks are inserted at the front and sampled first, while the oldest tasks are removed when the queue exceeds its capacity. This design reduces the need for additional rollouts while keeping recovery training focused on failures generated by recent policies.
 We conduct comprehensive experiments to assess the effectiveness of ReTerm. Built on Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) , our ReTerm-9B achieves 34.8% Pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and 58.4% Pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified ( Chowdhury et al., 2024 ) , establishing strong results on challenging terminal and coding tasks. Beyond these core evaluations, ReTerm also shows strong generalization across diverse out-of-domain benchmarks, improving over the base model by 20.0 points on AIME 2025 and 11.0 points on SpreadsheetBench Verified ( Ma et al., 2024 ) . Moreover, the same improvement trend persists under different agent harnesses, suggesting that our training induces transferable capabilities across interaction setups. To better understand where these gains come from, we identify five representative error categories in terminal interaction trajectories. Across all categories, ReTerm consistently exhibits higher recovery rates than the model trained by standard GRPO ( Guo et al., 2025 ) . Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
 • 
 We introduce ReTerm , a self-improving terminal agent developed through our recovery-oriented post-training framework. To support its training, we build a specialized data pipeline and construct ReTerm-10K, a high-quality dataset which offers effective recovery samples.
 • 
 To further improve recovery capability through online learning, we propose ReTerm-GRPO , which learns directly from failure states generated by the evolving policy and enables self-recovery capability to co-evolve with task-solving capability.
 • 
 Extensive experiments demonstrate that ReTerm achieves strong performance on multiple tough terminal and coding benchmarks and generalizes across various out-of-domain tasks and agent harnesses.
 2 Related Work
 Terminal Agent.
 Terminal agents, such as Codex ( OpenAI, 2025 ) and Claude Code ( Anthropic, 2025 ) , have demonstrated strong performance on long-horizon coding tasks. To assess such systems on complete workflows, Terminal-Bench 2.0 and OpenThoughts-TBLite provide standardized evaluations in diverse terminal environments ( Merrill et al., 2026 ; Raoof et al., 2026 ) . Recent work advances terminal agents’ ability by scaling verifiable training environments ( Gandhi et al., 2026 ; Shen et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Pi et al., 2026 ) and training agents with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards
 ( Yang et al., 2026b ) .
For instance, TMax generates large-scale
synthetic terminal tasks and explores a reinforcement
learning algorithm that filters unstable token updates, mitigating training instability in long-horizon terminal tasks ( Ivison et al., 2026 ) .
ECHO enhances terminal agents’
understanding of environment dynamics by introducing an
auxiliary observation prediction objective ( Shrivastava et al., 2026 ) .
However, prior work mainly focuses on improving task execution.
Our work complements these efforts by studying terminal agents’
recovery ability.
 Self-Correction.
 Self-correction has been widely studied as a way to improve language model outputs through iterative critique and revision
 ( Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Fan et al., 2026 ) .
Prior work has explored SFT and RL methods to enhance self-correction in static reasoning tasks
 ( Kumar et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) .
In interactive environments, however, self-correction is more challenging since erroneous actions may alter the environment and affect subsequent execution
 ( Yuan et al., 2025 ) .
Thus, recent work has explored self-correction training in terminal settings.
TermiGen synthesizes recovery trajectories through active error injection and uses them for SFT
 ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) .
However, hand-crafted errors may exhibit a distributional mismatch with agents’ own execution errors.
Our work differs by training terminal agents to recover from self-generated failure states.
 3 Method
 3.1 Recovery Dataset Formulation
 Figure 2: Overview of our data curation pipeline. Starting from an executable task, we collect various failed attempts induced by the base model. Then, a stronger model is asked to recover from the resulting failure state based on the limited information. For the generated recovery continuations, we finally apply four-step quality filtering and construct a high-quality dataset. 
 Training terminal agents to correct their execution errors requires demonstrations of successful recovery. As illustrated in Figure 2 , we construct such demonstrations through a three-stage pipeline based on the tasks sampled from TMax-15K ( Ivison et al., 2026 ) . First, we execute the base model on terminal tasks and preserve the interaction history and environment state of failed attempts. Second, we restore these failure states and use a stronger teacher model to generate recovery continuations. Finally, we filter the resulting recovery trajectories, retaining only high-quality ones for training.
 Failure State Collection.
 We first collect the failed attempts induced by the base model.
To guarantee the diversity of the failures, we use the base model (e.g., Qwen3.5-9B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) ) to sample multiple attempts for each task.
In practice, we execute each attempt in a task container hosted inside a virtual machine and record the full interaction history, including reasoning content, commands, and observations. To isolate the changes introduced by the task verifier, at the end of each attempt, we pause the virtual machine, take a snapshot of its running state and then resume it to run the verification. Based on the verification results, we retain the snapshots and interaction histories of failed attempts for the next stage and exclude successful attempts.
 Recovery Trajectory Generation.
 For each failed attempt, we then restore the saved snapshot and employ DeepSeek-V4-Pro ( Xu et al., 2026 ) to continue the task conditioned on the original task instruction and the student’s interaction history. Specifically, the teacher model is only informed that the previous attempt failed but receives no detailed feedback from the hidden tests,
so it must infer the unresolved problems from the execution history and the restored environment.
To guide this process, we provide a recovery workflow that encourages the teacher to inspect the inherited state, diagnose the failure, plan and execute repairs, and verify the resulting work.
After completing the recovery attempt, we record the teacher’s continuation as a candidate recovery trajectory for subsequent filtering. The full recovery workflow is provided in the appendix.
 Recovery Trajectory Filtering.
 The resulting candidate recovery trajectories are then filtered along four dimensions to ensure recovery quality.
(1) Verifier filtering retains only recoveries that pass the task verifier.
(2) Format filtering removes recoveries with malformed messages or tool calls.
(3) Compliance filtering excludes recoveries containing actions that explicitly violate task constraints.
(4) Efficiency filtering discards recoveries including meaningless action loops in which the agent repeats unsuccessful commands without effective adjustment.
The verifier and format filtering are implemented with deterministic rule-based checks.
For compliance and efficiency, we first apply rule-based checks to flag trajectories with potential compliance or efficiency issues. We then use DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ( Xu et al., 2026 ) to assess each flagged case in context and determine whether it should be filtered out.
Finally, we obtain roughly 10K high-quality recovery trajectories after the four-step filtering.
Detailed review prompts are provided in the appendix.

 Recovery-Targeted Supervision.
 We next use the filtered recovery trajectories for SFT.
Although the teacher model is guided by a structured recovery workflow during data generation, we remove this additional guidance to reduce reliance on this detailed instruction and retain only a minimal recovery prompt.
Accordingly, the resulting training examples take the task instruction, the student’s failed interaction history, and the recovery prompt as input, while the loss is computed solely on the teacher-generated tokens:
 | 
 $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,h_{f},r,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ReTerm}}}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{A}(y)}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\left(y_{t}\mid x,h_{f},r,y_{<t}\right)\right],$ | 
 | 
 (1) | 
 where $x$ is the task instruction, $h_{f}$ is the student’s interaction history in the failed attempt,
 $r$ is the recovery prompt and $y$ is the teacher’s subsequent recovery trajectory.
 $\mathcal{A}(y)$ denotes the positions of teacher-generated tokens.
All other tokens are masked from the loss.
 3.2 ReTerm-GRPO
 Figure 4: An illustration of our proposed ReTerm-GRPO. 
 The recovery demonstrations used for SFT are constructed from failures generated by a fixed base model. As the policy evolves during RL, however, its failure distribution may shift beyond that represented in the fixed dataset. We therefore introduce ReTerm-GRPO, which allows self-recovery capability to adapt alongside task-solving capability.
 Algorithm Overview.
 Figure 4 illustrates the overall training procedure of ReTerm-GRPO.
We start from an original task pool $\mathcal{T}=\{(x,s_{0})\}$ , where $x$ denotes the task instruction and $s_{0}$ denotes its initial environment state.
For each original task, the policy generates $G$ independent rollouts from $s_{0}$ :
 | 
 $\tau_{i}^{O}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,s_{0}),\qquad R_{i}^{O}=V_{x}(s_{i}^{O}),\quad i=1,\ldots,G,$ | 
 | 
 (2) | 
 where $\tau_{i}^{O}$ denotes the $i$ -th rollout for an original task, $s_{i}^{O}$ is its final environment state, $V_{x}$ is the task verifier, and $R_{i}^{O}$ is the corresponding verifier reward.
For each original task group containing at least one failed rollout, we sample one failed rollout and preserve its interaction history $h_{f}$ and resulting environment state $s_{f}$ .
Together with the original task instruction $x$ , we define the corresponding recovery task as $(x,h_{f},s_{f})$ , which requires the policy to recover from the resulting failure state and complete the original task.
We store the constructed recovery tasks in a recovery task queue, from which they are sampled in subsequent training iterations.
For each recovery task sampled from the queue, we provide a recovery prompt $r$ and generate $G$ independent recovery rollouts from $s_{f}$ :
 | 
 $\tau_{i}^{R}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,h_{f},r,s_{f}),\qquad R_{i}^{R}=V_{x}(s_{i}^{R}),\quad i=1,\ldots,G,$ | 
 | 
 (3) | 
 where $\tau_{i}^{R}$ denotes the $i$ -th rollout for a recovery task, and $s_{i}^{R}$ and $R_{i}^{R}$ are its final environment state and verifier reward, respectively.
For both original and recovery tasks, the verifier rewards of the $G$ rollouts are normalized within each group to derive group-relative advantages.
We subsequently optimize the policy jointly over the two types of groups.
 Recovery Task Queue.
 Since a single training batch may not contain enough failed rollouts, we maintain a recovery task queue with capacity $K$ to retain recovery tasks across training steps. The stored tasks supplement newly constructed recovery tasks, allowing recovery training to proceed even when the current batch produces few or no failures, without requiring additional original task rollouts solely for failure collection. To keep the sampled tasks relevant to the evolving policy, newly constructed tasks are inserted at the front of the queue and sampled before older ones. When the queue exceeds its capacity, the oldest tasks are removed, limiting the accumulation of failures generated by much earlier policies. By retaining recovery tasks across iterations while favoring recent ones, the queue reduces rollout collection overhead and limits the mismatch between the sampled recovery tasks and the current policy’s failure distribution.
 Policy Optimization.
 ReTerm-GRPO computes group-relative advantages and the corresponding GRPO objective ( Guo et al., 2025 ; Feng et al., 2026a ) separately for original and recovery task groups, and then combines the two objectives for joint policy optimization.
For a training group $g$ of type $c\in\{\mathrm{O},\mathrm{R}\}$ , where $\mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{R}$ denote original and recovery task groups, respectively, let $\mathcal{A}_{g,i}^{c}$ denote the model-generated token positions included in optimization for the $i$ -th rollout.
The training objective is
 | 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{g}^{c}(\theta)\!=\!\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\!\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{g,i}^{c}|}\!\sum_{t\in\mathcal{A}_{g,i}^{c}}\!\!\min\!\Big\{$ | 
 $\displaystyle\rho_{g,i,t}^{c}(\theta)\hat{A}_{g,i}^{c},\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho_{g,i,t}^{c}(\theta),1\!-\!\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1\!+\!\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}\right)\!\hat{A}_{g,i}^{c}\Big\}\Bigg].$ | 
 | 
 (4) | 
 For each rollout, the policy ratio and group-relative advantage are defined as
 | 
 $\rho_{g,i,t}^{c}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{g,i,t}^{c}\mid h_{g,i,t}^{c})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{g,i,t}^{c}\mid h_{g,i,t}^{c})},\qquad\hat{A}_{g,i}^{c}=\frac{R_{g,i}^{c}-\operatorname{mean}(R_{g,j}^{c})}{\operatorname{std}(R_{g,j}^{c})+\delta}.$ | 
 | 
 (5) | 
 Here, $y_{g,i,t}^{c}$ denotes the model-generated token at position $t$ , $h_{g,i,t}^{c}$ denotes its preceding context, and $R_{g,i}^{c}$ is the verifier reward of the corresponding rollout.
Let $M_{\mathrm{O}}$ and $M_{\mathrm{R}}$ denote the numbers of original task and recovery task groups.
The overall ReTerm-GRPO objective is
 | 
 $\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{ReTerm}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{M_{\mathrm{O}}+M_{\mathrm{R}}}\left(\sum_{g=1}^{M_{\mathrm{O}}}\mathcal{J}_{g}^{\mathrm{O}}(\theta)+\sum_{g=1}^{M_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathcal{J}_{g}^{\mathrm{R}}(\theta)\right).$ | 
 | 
 (6) | 
 In this way, ReTerm-GRPO enables the model to continuously adapt to its evolving failure modes, allowing self-recovery capability to co-evolve with task-solving ability throughout training.
 4 Experiment
 4.1 Experimental Setup
 Training Details.
 Our training is performed on 16 NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs.
We use Qwen3.5-9B as the base model and partition ReTerm-10K into 6K trajectories for SFT and 4K task instances for RL.
We perform full-parameter SFT using AdamW with a global batch size of 16 and a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-6}$ .
We subsequently initialize ReTerm-GRPO from the SFT checkpoint.
For every update, we sample 8 original task groups and 2 recovery task groups.
Each GRPO group contains 16 rollouts and therefore the global batch size is 160.
We use AdamW with a learning rate of $5\times 10^{-7}$ .
Rollouts are sampled with a maximum generation length of 8,192 tokens, a 65k context window, and at most 32 interaction turns.
 Benchmarks.
 We evaluate ReTerm on seven benchmarks spanning a variety of domains.
First, we use OpenThoughts-TBLite ( OpenThoughts-Agent Team et al., 2026 ) and Terminal-Bench 2.0 ( Merrill et al., 2026 ) , which contain diverse executable tasks requiring long-horizon interaction with terminal environments.
We further evaluate on SWE-bench Verified ( Chowdhury et al., 2024 ) , a human-verified benchmark for resolving real-world software issues in GitHub repositories.
To assess out-of-domain generalization, we include AIME 2024, AIME 2025, Aider Polyglot ( Aider, 2024 ) and SpreadsheetBench Verified ( Ma et al., 2024 ) .
 Evaluation Protocol.
 We implement and use a lightweight ReAct-style harness that exposes a Bash interface.
We allocate a 256k-token budget per task, set the maximum generation length to 16k tokens, and use a temperature of 1.0, top- $p$ of 1.0, with the task seed fixed at 0 during inference.
Qwen3.5-9B is evaluated under exactly the same harness and evaluation configuration as ReTerm.
For other models, we report results from their original papers or official releases when available.

 4.2 Main Results
 Table 1 reports the main results across the three core agentic benchmarks.
ReTerm-9B achieves 60.6% Pass@1 on OpenThoughts-TBLite, 34.8% on Terminal-Bench 2.0, and 58.4% on SWE-bench Verified, corresponding to gains of 16.4, 14.6, and 13.2 points over its Qwen3.5-9B base model, respectively.
At the same model scale, ReTerm-9B consistently outperforms TMax-9B across all three benchmarks.
On SWE-bench Verified, it also surpasses Socratic-SWE, a self-evolving software engineering agent built on Qwen3.5-9B.
Despite its smaller model size, ReTerm-9B further outperforms several 32B specialized agents, including Terminal-World-32B on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and OpenThinkerAgent-32B on SWE-bench Verified.
Notably, it also substantially outperforms TermiGen-32B, which is trained with synthesized error-correction trajectories, on the terminal benchmarks.
Overall, these results demonstrate strong and consistent gains across challenging terminal tasks and repository-level software engineering.
 4.3 Generalization
 In this section, we examine whether the gains of ReTerm generalize beyond its default training setup.
Specifically, we evaluate two aspects: robustness across different agent harnesses and transfer to out-of-domain task distributions.
 Across Agent Harnesses.
 An agent’s performance can depend on the harness that mediates interaction with the environment, including its prompting format, tool interface, and interaction protocol.
Therefore, we examine whether ReTerm’s improvement is tied to the particular interaction setup.
To isolate harness effects from task-distribution changes, we keep the Terminal-Bench 2.0 tasks fixed and evaluate both Qwen3.5-9B and ReTerm-9B under four harnesses: our training harness (ReAct), Terminus-2 ( Merrill et al., 2026 ) , mini-SWE-agent ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , and OpenHands ( Wang et al., 2025 ) .

 In this section, we compare four training settings to examine the contribution of different stages in ReTerm: (1) Base, the original Qwen3.5-9B model without post-training; (2) SFT, which applies supervised fine-tuning only; (3) GRPO, which performs standard GRPO training instead of ReTerm-GRPO; and (4) ReTerm-GRPO, our proposed recovery-oriented RL method.

 4.5 Error Analysis
 Figure 5: Recovery rates after encountering different error types. 
 To understand how our method improves terminal agents, we analyze trajectories from the base model, the GRPO-trained model, and the ReTerm-GRPO-trained model on Terminal-Bench 2.0. We define five error types: (1) Planning Error : misunderstanding requirements or choosing incorrect strategies; (2) Tool Use Error : mistaken use of terminal commands, such as invalid commands and improper arguments; (3) Implementation Error : producing incorrect or incomplete task artifacts; (4) Verification Error : failing to detect errors through testing and debugging; and (5) Resource Management Error : leaving the task unfinished due to poor management of time, interaction budget, or execution state. We employ DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ( Xu et al., 2026 ) as an LLM judge to assess recovery outcomes after the agent encounters each of the five error types, followed by manual verification.
Figure 5 shows that ReTerm-GRPO achieves higher recovery rates across all categories than both the base model and the GRPO baseline. Despite these gains, recovery remains challenging: except for tool use error, the recovery rates of ReTerm-GRPO remain at or below 32% across all other categories. This suggests that terminal agents still fail to recover from most encountered errors, leaving substantial room for further improving recovery capability.
 4.6 Training Curves
 Figure 6: Mean reward curves during ReTerm-GRPO training. 
 Figure 6 shows the optimization dynamics of ReTerm-GRPO on original and recovery tasks. Both groups improve rapidly during the early stage of RL training and stabilize as training proceeds. Notably, the recovery task group exhibits a larger improvement from its initial performance. At the same time, reward on original tasks also improves and remains stable, suggesting that recovery-oriented examples provide an effective learning signal and can be jointly optimized with original tasks without destabilizing the training process.
 5 Conclusion
 In this work, we present ReTerm, a self-improving terminal agent trained to recover from self-generated execution errors. To support training, we construct ReTerm-10K, a high-quality dataset that provides effective recovery demonstrations for SFT cold start. We further propose ReTerm-GRPO, an online RL algorithm that enables self-recovery capability to co-evolve with task-solving capability. Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. We hope this work takes a step toward developing more capable and robust terminal agents through recovery-driven self-improvement.
