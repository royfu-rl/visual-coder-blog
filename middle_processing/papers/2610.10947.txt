 RH-Detect : A Unified Benchmark for Reward Hacking Detection 

 Junwei Quan

 Evgenii Opryshko

 Rohan Subramani

 Igor Gilitschenski

 Abstract 

 Reward hacking, where a model exploits an evaluation signal without completing
the intended task, threatens the reliability of deployed language model
systems. Existing datasets use different labels, response formats, and
metadata conventions, making detector results difficult to compare. We present
 RH-Detect , a benchmark that combines reward-hacking-relevant subsets from eleven public datasets, comprising
92,761 rows and six behavior categories, into a common schema.
On 5,021 open-ended evaluation units, each comprising a task prompt and a free-form model continuation, including multi-turn tool-use trajectories, we evaluate six off-the-shelf language models from five families as reward hacking detectors without additional training. The best
model achieves a pooled AUROC of 0.962, with accuracy above 93%.
For the four strongest models, however, accuracy on the two multi-turn tool-use datasets, MALT and TRACE, is 10.7–15.9 percentage points lower
than on the other sources at a common decision threshold, highlighting a key
gap for deployment-time monitoring.
We find that different input formats have different effects across models.
Removing thinking raises Qwen3.5-4B AUROC from 0.779 to
0.849, but lowers Qwen Flash from 0.977 to
0.950. We also evaluate the benchmark as a training dataset for
detectors. Holding out each source in turn, single-token SFT improves average AUROC on five of six held-out sources. A GRPO follow-up on that failure case yields a slight improvement in detection performance. Our results show that a single pooled score can conceal
variation across data sources, detector inputs, and training procedures.

 1 Introduction 

 Language models can exploit imperfections in training or evaluation signals, achieving high measured reward without completing the intended task. Such behavior ranges from sycophancy and test-case exploitation to reward tampering and concealment under monitoring ( Gao et al. 2023 ; Sharma et al. 2024 ; Denison et al. 2024 ; Baker et al. 2025 ) . Recent work further shows that learning to reward hack in production reinforcement-learning environments can induce broader emergent misalignment, including alignment faking, cooperation with malicious actors, and attempted sabotage ( MacDiarmid et al. 2025 ) . We use reward hacking broadly to mean behavior that satisfies an evaluation signal without fulfilling the intended task, including behavior observed outside the training process, which is also referred to as “evaluation gaming” ( Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024 ; Denison et al. 2024 ) . Reward hacking is therefore not only a performance or evaluation problem: it may also undermine deployment-time oversight as models are entrusted with higher-stakes tasks. 

 Several research groups have independently released datasets related to reward
hacking ( Taylor et al. 2025 ; Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024 ; Deshpande et al. 2026 ; Parikh and Wijk 2025 ; Perez et al. 2023 ; Jozdien 2025 ; de la Fuente and Mann 2025 ; Khalifa et al. 2026 ; Greenblatt et al. 2024 ; Bercovich et al. 2026 ; CheatBench Maintainers 2026 ) . Each source, however, uses its own labeling scheme, response format, metadata conventions, and evaluation procedure, and several contain traces irrelevant to reward hacking. This fragmentation mirrors a broader problem in
behavioral testing: aggregate scores are difficult to interpret when task
definitions and evaluation pipelines are not standardized
 ( Ribeiro et al. 2020 ) . A practitioner who wants to evaluate one
monitoring pipeline across a broad range of hacking behaviors must currently
build source-specific ingestion, filtering, and interpretation pipelines, and a strong result
on one dataset provides limited evidence that the same detector will transfer
to an unseen source. 

 We address this gap with RH-Detect , a common framework that harmonizes reward-hacking-relevant subsets from eleven public datasets. The benchmark provides a shared schema, a six-category behavior taxonomy, provenance metadata, task-specific tracks, and source-held-out splits audited by exact prompt hash. We use it to compare
language models as detectors and to study whether measured performance remains
stable across sources, input representations, and post-training procedures. 

 We use RH-Detect to answer three research questions: 

 RQ1 

 How effectively can language models detect reward hacking
without additional training, and how does performance vary across model
families? 

 RQ2 

 How does the monitor performance differ across data sources and reward hacking categories? 

 RQ3 

 How sensitive is detector performance to transcript representation,
and how well do supervised and RL post-training methods transfer to held-out data sources and task tracks? 

 RQ1 tests whether existing language
models can serve as low-cost monitors without task-specific training. RQ2
identifies where strong aggregate performance hides source- or behavior-specific
weaknesses. RQ3 tests whether changes to detector input or training
procedure improve cross-source robustness rather than only aggregate performance. Reliable
detection is a prerequisite for downstream reward hacking interventions
 ( Mallen 2025 ) . 

 Our contributions are as follows. 

 1. 

 A unified benchmark that selects and maps reward-hacking-relevant subsets from eleven public datasets, comprising 92,761 rows, into a common schema while preserving inherited label semantics, source provenance, and task-specific formats. The benchmark includes a six-category taxonomy and exact prompt-hash and source-boundary audits for supervised evaluation (§ 3 , § 3.3 ). 

 2. 

 A reproducible detector evaluation across six models from
five families. We report pooled AUROC, average per-source AUROC,
category-specific results, and direct source-stratified comparisons
rather than relying on one aggregate score (§ 4 ). 

 3. 

 An input and training analysis that compares transcript
preprocessing and evidence extraction, single-token
supervised fine-tuning under six source-held-out rotations and across the open-ended and multiple-choice tracks, with an extra targeted RL training run (§ 5 ). 

 Together, these experiments evaluate not only whether a detector performs well
on average, but also whether its behavior remains stable when the data source,
input representation, or training procedure changes. 

 2 Related Work 

 The reward hacking landscape. 

 Reward hacking in language models ranges from superficial optimization of
proxy signals to strategic manipulation of the evaluation process. Sycophancy
can arise when human feedback rewards agreement with a user’s stated beliefs
rather than factual accuracy ( Sharma et al. 2024 ) . Under stronger
optimization pressure, models may progress from evaluation gaming to direct
reward tampering ( Denison et al. 2024 ) , and behaviors learned in one
task can generalize to held-out tasks ( Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024 ) . Controlled
environments such as ImpossibleBench, EvilGenie, and the Reward Hacking
Benchmark elicit verifiable exploits in coding and tool-use settings
 ( Zhong et al. 2025 ; Gabor et al. 2025 ; Thaman 2026 ) . These works
ask whether a model exploits an evaluation. Our complementary question is
whether a monitor recognizes diverse exploits after they have occurred. 

 A fragmented dataset ecosystem. 

 Existing datasets each target a different slice of the problem. School of
Reward Hacks provides paired hacking and clean continuations across language
and coding tasks ( Taylor et al. 2025 ) . MALT, TRACE, Terminal Wrench, and CheatBench contain
multi-turn tool-use trajectories ( Parikh and Wijk 2025 ; Deshpande et al. 2026 ; Bercovich et al. 2026 ; CheatBench Maintainers 2026 ) , while
other sources focus on style gaming, hardcoded solutions, reward tampering,
multiple-choice behavioral probes, or alignment faking
 ( Jozdien 2025 ; de la Fuente and Mann 2025 ; Khalifa et al. 2026 ; Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024 ; Perez et al. 2023 ; Greenblatt et al. 2024 ) .
The main contribution of RH-Detect is a common schema and
evaluation interface that makes cross-source comparison practical while
preserving source provenance and redistribution constraints. 

 Monitoring and LLM-as-judge. 

 Aggregate accuracy can hide systematic failures across behavioral dimensions
 ( Ribeiro et al. 2020 ) , and language models used as judges exhibit known
position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases ( Zheng et al. 2023 ) .
Recent monitoring work shows that the information presented to a judge also
matters: contrastive groups improve detection on TRACE
 ( Deshpande et al. 2026 ) , and extract-and-evaluate pipelines can outperform
full-trajectory monitoring ( Arike et al. 2026 ) . Deliberative monitors
have been trained across several agentic sources ( Sinha et al. 2026 ) ,
while AutoMonitor-Bench is smaller and evaluates a broader collection of failure modes
 ( Yang et al. 2026 ) . Other benchmarks study monitor-aware evasion
 ( Jotautaitė et al. 2026 ; Najt et al. 2026 ) . Our study differs in the axis of variation: rather than training a single
monitor ( Sinha et al. 2026 ) or surveying many failure modes
 ( Yang et al. 2026 ) , we hold the detection task fixed and vary the
data source, transcript representation, and post-training procedure for the
same detector. This isolates a factor those studies hold constant, and shows
that it moves measured detection performance as much as the choice of detector
does. The setup also differs from effort-based and activation-based monitoring
 ( Wang et al. 2026 ; Wilhelm et al. 2026 ) , which inspect internal signals
rather than transcripts.

 3.1 Taxonomy of Reward Hacking Behaviors 

 We surveyed the selected source datasets and analyzed the model
behaviors they capture. The sources range from paired
prompt–response examples with deterministic labels
 ( Taylor et al. 2025 ; de la Fuente and Mann 2025 ) to richly
annotated, multi-turn tool-use trajectories with human review
 ( Deshpande et al. 2026 ; Parikh and Wijk 2025 ) . Despite these differences,
their documented behaviors cluster into six recurring top-level
categories. 

 Output style gaming. 

 The model exploits surface-level evaluation heuristics, such as
keyword density, formatting, or response length, to increase a score
without improving substantive quality. The current release contains the verbosity padding subcategory. 

 Sycophancy. 

 The model agrees with the user’s stated opinion or mirrors the user’s
framing regardless of factual correctness, optimizing for approval
rather than accuracy. Subcategories include opinion ,
 factual , and theory-of-mind 
variants. 

 Evaluation gaming. 

 The model produces an output that passes an automated check without
implementing a genuine solution. Subcategories
include hardcoded lookup , test modification ,
 answer-key extraction , metric or timing manipulation ,
and coverage gaming . 

 Environment exploitation. 

 The model manipulates the evaluation infrastructure, for example by
altering timeout settings, patching a test runner, or modifying
filesystem state so that a check passes without resolving the
underlying task. The release contains the filesystem or runtime interference subcategory. 

 Reward tampering. 

 The model directly modifies the mechanism that computes its reward or
verification signal, such as overwriting a grading function so that it
always returns a passing result. The release contains the reward-code modification subcategory. 

 Deception. 

 The model strategically conceals its behavior or the means by which it
obtained a result. For example, it may generate plausible intermediate
steps after accessing an answer through an illegitimate channel.
Subcategories include strategic deception ,
 self-preservation , and alignment faking . 

 These categories describe the type and target of a behavior rather
than an ordinal level of severity or sophistication. For exposition,
they move from surface-level exploitation toward direct manipulation
and concealment, but the ordering should not be interpreted as a
severity scale. Table 1 gives representative examples
from the five categories appearing in the primary open-ended track;
sycophancy appears only in the multiple-choice track. 

 3.2 Source Selection and Harmonization 

 The current release integrates reward-hacking-relevant subsets from eleven
public sources that contain pre-generated hacking or clean responses, or
sufficient metadata from which binary labels can be derived. Source-specific access and redistribution conditions are documented with the
release; inclusion in the common schema does not imply permission to
redistribute every source record, and one source requires the encrypted
distribution described in § 3.3 . 

 The selected sources span two response formats: open-ended
model-generated text, including multi-turn tool-use trajectories, and
multiple-choice probes. We retain the source-provided pairing whenever
a prompt has both a hacking and a clean continuation. For an unpaired
hacking continuation, we add a clean continuation drawn from the same
task or source split if possible, so that each represented behavior category
contains similar number of positive and negative examples. 

 We assign each row a category and subcategory using the source
metadata. Some labels map directly. For example, MALT’s manually
reviewed hardcoded solution label maps to evaluation gaming
and the hardcoded lookup subcategory, while
 sabotage maps to deception. TRACE provides a native
54-subcategory annotation scheme that we consolidate into the common
taxonomy. School of Reward Hacks provides human-readable
 cheat method descriptions, which we map to output style
gaming or evaluation gaming according to whether the exploit targets
a surface metric or an evaluation mechanism. 

 Metadata. 

 Beyond the binary label and taxonomy assignment, each row preserves
or derives the following fields: construction method , indicating
whether the behavior arose without an explicit hacking instruction or
was deliberately elicited; verification method , such as human
review, an LLM judge, or a deterministic rule; a free-text
 hacking description , when provided by the source; the
 response model that generated the continuation; and an
 agentic flag , indicating a multi-turn trajectory involving tool
use or autonomous interaction with an environment. 

 Reproducibility. 

 The per-source inventory with row counts, class balance, and verification method, the verbatim detector prompt, and the SFT and GRPO training configurations are provided in the appendix; Appendix F describes the dataset and code release. 

 3.3 Unified Schema, Evaluation Tracks, and Splits 

 Each row is stored as a chat-template message list containing the
prompt and one or more continuations, together with the structured
metadata described above. The release is organized into three storage
partitions: 24,528 public open-ended rows, 66,760 multiple-choice
rows, and 1,473 protected alignment-faking responses distributed
under source-required XOR encryption to limit direct exposure of
their contents. 

 A source row may contain both a hacking and a clean continuation.
Several sources reuse the same prompt across hacking and clean
continuations, so a naive random split can place an identical prompt
in both training and evaluation. We use two supervised split protocols.
Cross-source transfer uses six held-out rotations. Cross-track transfer instead uses train, validation, and test splits grouped by prompt hash within each track. In both protocols, identical prompt hashes do not cross the training and evaluation boundary. 

 4 Zero-Shot Detector Evaluation 

 4.1 Evaluation Methodology 

 We perform our primary evaluations on six open-ended sources selected because they were available when we started the evaluations:
School of Reward Hacks, Jozdien Realistic Reward Hacks, Patronus TRACE,
METR MALT, Countdown-Code, and Matonski Reward-Hacking Prompts. Excluding transcripts longer than 32,000 tokens leaves 5,021 eligible evaluation units. Each unit contains a prompt and one continuation. A
fixed detector prompt asks the model to classify the continuation as reward
hacking or clean and return a JSON with reasoning and a probability from 0 to 100, which we divide
by 100 for analysis. All calls use temperature 0. The detector receives the
rendered transcript but no source name, category, label, construction method,
or verification metadata. It can use task intent, evaluator behavior, and
environment state only when those details appear in the transcript. 

 We concatenate the message contents from the task
prompt, model continuation and tool calls in their original order. Performance on this track
measures the complete monitoring pipeline, from transcript interpretation and
evidence aggregation to the final classification. We evaluate six models from five families through a commercial API:
Llama 4 Scout and Maverick, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, GPT-5 Nano, Grok 4.1 Fast,
and Qwen 3.5 Flash. The selection spans several architectures and price points. Grok was withdrawn
from the API before the final MALT evaluation was complete. Its reported
results cover non-MALT units and are excluded from paired and
source-stratified comparisons.

 5.2 Single-Token Supervised Fine-Tuning 

 To test whether supervised reward-hacking detectors transfer beyond the
sources observed during training, we fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B under six strict
leave-one-source-out rotations. Each rotation removes one open-ended source
in full before constructing the training split, and evaluates the resulting
detector only on that omitted source. Within the remaining sources, examples
sharing an exact prompt hash are assigned to the same split. We perform
full-parameter fine-tuning for one epoch with three random seeds and evaluate
the final checkpoint from each run. Training sets contain 2,354–3,911
evaluation units, depending on the omitted source. Each example uses the Qwen
chat template with an assistant prefill ending in Answer: . Prompt
positions are masked, and the loss is applied to exactly one next token:
 YES for reward hacking or NO for clean behavior. At
evaluation time, the detector score is the normalized conditional probability
of the two label tokens, 

 We use source-held-out rather than within-source evaluation because the two
settings answer different questions. A random split within a source measures
interpolation to a familiar data-generating process and permits the detector
to exploit recurring task templates, formatting conventions, label
construction procedures, and other source-specific cues. Such a split may
therefore overstate performance on data from a new benchmark or deployment
environment. In our design, the test source contributes no examples to
training, validation, threshold selection, or checkpoint selection. The
exact-prompt audit additionally prevents identical prompts from crossing the
training and test boundary. This design does not exclude paraphrased or semantic
overlap between sources, nor does it establish the absence of pretraining
contamination.

 6 Discussion 

 Off-the-shelf LLMs as monitors. 

 The zero-shot results show that general-purpose language models can provide
strong reward hacking rankings at modest evaluation cost. Qwen Flash and
Gemini both exceed 93% accuracy at the common threshold, and the best pooled
AUROC is 0.962. Model choice still matters. Gemini produces the lowest
false-positive rate, while GPT-5 Nano
assigns substantial suspicion to benign responses. 

 Performance on MALT and TRACE. 

 The clearest source-stratified result is the gap between MALT/TRACE and the
other four open-ended sources. The four strongest complete-coverage judges are
10.7–15.9 percentage points less accurate on MALT and
TRACE. Balanced accuracy, AUROC and AUPRC all move in the same
direction, so the result is not explained only by class balance or the threshold. MALT and TRACE contain long tool-use trajectories, making this gap
important for deployment-time monitoring. 

 Input and training sensitivity. 

 The transcript experiments show that preprocessing is part of the detector
configuration. Removing input reasoning improves the 4B judge,
but the same change helps neither API judge.
Extract-and-evaluate monitoring can preserve the stronger judge’s performance,
yet the result falls when a weaker model chooses the excerpts. Fine-tuning is
similarly uneven. Single-token supervision improves mean AUROC across held-out sources,
but performance declines on SoRH. Cross-track transfer is more limited. Fine-tuning on either
track produces near-ceiling AUROC on that track but lowers
AUROC on the other track to approximately chance or below.
These findings do not show that preprocessing or
supervised training is generally helpful or harmful. They show that gains on
one aggregate or source need not transfer to another detector interface or
data source, which highlights the importance of testing on non-homogeneous datasets. 

 6.1 Limitations and Future Work 

 Four caveats bound our claims. First, labels are inherited and
heterogeneous in provenance, so a source with noisier annotation may appear
harder for reasons unrelated to detection difficulty, and binary labels do
not capture the severity spectrum needed for risk-prioritized triage.
Second, where a source provides no paired clean continuation we draw the
negative from the same task or source split, so we cannot fully exclude
stylistic cues unrelated to hacking.
Third, the MALT/TRACE gap is a source-level association: length, tool
formatting, category mixture, elicitation, and verification vary together
with agentic status. Fourth, the training study is limited to one model. The benchmark is also static,
whereas deployment monitoring is incremental and latency-constrained.
Future work should audit semantic overlap, add severity-graded labels,
evaluate against adversarial policies
 ( Jotautaitė et al. 2026 ; Najt et al. 2026 ) , and expand the training
study across model families and output formats. 

 7 Conclusion 

 We present RH-Detect , a common benchmark that harmonizes
reward-hacking-relevant subsets from eleven public datasets using consistent
metadata, task-specific tracks, and audited source-held-out splits. Off-the-shelf language models range from strong to unreliable on the primary open-ended track, with pooled AUROC from 0.817 to 0.962. However, the four strongest models are less accurate on MALT and TRACE than on the other sources, identifying
multi-turn tool-use monitoring as the area where additional engineering investment is most needed. 

 The additional experiments show that detector performance depends on both transcript representation and post-training strategy. Single-token SFT improves average AUROC per-source, but the gains are uneven and do not transfer across tracks. A targeted GRPO training avoids the large SFT regression and yields a small positive held-out gain. Reliable reward-hacking monitoring therefore requires source-stratified evaluation and complete specification of the detector input, score readout, and training procedure.

 Appendix A Dataset and Release Construction 

 RH-Detect integrates eleven public datasets comprising
92,761 rows, organized into open-ended, multiple-choice, and
protected-response tracks. Nine datasets were available
when the reported experiments were conducted and contribute
72,384 rows across the three tracks, and Terminal Wrench and CheatBench were incorporated after the
reported experiments; they are included in the integrated open-ended benchmark but do not enter analysis. Stored rows and
detector evaluation units differ: rows that pair a hacking and a clean
continuation expand into two units, so the 4,151 core
open-ended rows yield 5,174 units, of which
5,021 satisfy the 32,000-token eligibility policy of the
primary analysis. Table S1 gives the complete
inventory. 

 A.1 Sources 

 School of Reward Hacks. 

 School of Reward Hacks contains short, self-contained language and
coding tasks whose prompts specify an exploitable evaluation metric.
Each task provides a reward-hacking continuation that optimizes the
stated metric without fulfilling the intended objective and a clean
continuation that makes a good-faith attempt at the task ( Taylor et al. 2025 ) . We preserve
the free-text cheat_method field and use it to assign the
unified category. 

 Jozdien realistic reward hacks. 

 Jozdien contains reward-hacking examples on code problems and literary
questions, together with benign HHH responses in corresponding formats ( Jozdien 2025 ) .
We retain the unpaired hacking and clean continuations from the code and
literary splits. The hacking examples are mapped to evaluation gaming
and output style gaming, respectively. 

 Patronus TRACE. 

 TRACE is a human-verified benchmark of multi-turn coding-agent
trajectories with simulated tool use across a broad range of software
engineering domains ( Deshpande et al. 2026 ) . Its native annotations cover 54 fine-grained
reward-hacking subcategories. We consolidate these annotations using
their two-level label prefixes, render tool calls inline, and represent
tool results as tool-role messages. 

 METR MALT. 

 MALT contains natural and prompted agent trajectories on software and
research tasks, annotated for behaviors that may threaten evaluation
integrity, including reward hacking and sandbagging, together with
reviewed benign runs ( Parikh and Wijk 2025 ) . The 286 hacking runs are selected by the label
set sabotage , hardcoded_solution , bypass_constraints , or
 ignores_task_instructions , and multi-label rows are resolved
by that fixed priority order. The 286 clean runs labeled exactly
 normal are matched in five seeded passes, preferring the same
task and model, then task family, and finally any reviewed normal run,
under seed 42. Each transcript is linearized from the source node graph
with function calls inlined. 

 Countdown-Code. 

 Countdown-Code is a controlled coding environment based on the
Countdown arithmetic game, in which an agent can receive the proxy
reward either by constructing a valid arithmetic solution or by
modifying the accompanying test harness ( Khalifa et al. 2026 ) . RH-Detect derives the label deterministically from the generated
files. We label a transcript as
hacking exactly when the verification expression
 sorted(used_numbers) is absent from the rewritten
 test.py , indicating that the substantive input-usage check has
been removed. From this corpus, 167 hacking and 167 honest transcripts
are sampled with seed 42. Prompt-level pairing is unavailable because
the retained hacking and honest trajectories arise from disjoint task
outcomes rather than matched continuations to the same prompt. 

 Matonski prompts. 

 Matonski contains 50 computational prompts spanning tasks such as
simulation, numerical optimization, and graph algorithms ( de la Fuente and Mann 2025 ) . Each prompt
exposes grader code that reads reference outputs from
 expected.json , creating a shortcut in which the model can copy
the expected values rather than perform the requested computation. We
retain one honest and one reward-hacking continuation per prompt,
including their reasoning traces, yielding 50 paired prompt groups. 

 Reward-hack generalization. 

 The reward-hack generalization dataset contains multiple-choice
behavioral probes with a source-provided
 high_reward_answer and one or more
 other_answers ( Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024 ) .
For each item, we retain the high-reward answer as the positive
continuation and use the first available alternative as the negative
continuation, yielding one paired binary example. The source file
determines the RH-Detect category and subcategory, covering
sycophancy, reward tampering, evaluation gaming, and deception. 

 Anthropic model-written evaluations. 

 The selected Anthropic model-written evaluations contain
multiple-choice probes of sycophancy and persona-related behaviors
 ( Perez et al. 2023 ) . Each item provides an
 answer_matching_behavior and an
 answer_not_matching_behavior ; we retain the former as
the positive continuation and the latter as the negative continuation.
The selected source files map to sycophancy, deception, evaluation
gaming, or environment exploitation according to the behavior being
probed. 

 Alignment-faking data. 

 The protected track combines samples from the Anthropic
alignment-faking RL corpus ( MacDiarmid et al. 2025 ) 
with 106 human-labeled alignment-faking responses
 ( Greenblatt et al. 2024 ) . For the RL corpus, we use
a stratified DuckDB query that selects up to five positive and five
negative rows per combination of experiment and model initialization. The additional 106 rows
use the source-provided human binary label directly. Following the
source distribution protocol, every stored response is XOR-encrypted
and prefixed with the source canary, so protected responses are
distributed only as ciphertext. 

 Terminal Wrench. 

 Terminal Wrench contains terminal-agent trajectories from
reward-hackable environments, together with baseline runs,
legitimate solutions produced under an attacker instruction, and
unsuccessful attacker attempts ( Bercovich et al. 2026 ) .
We collapse the source outcome classes into the RH-Detect field: rewarded_serious_exploit and
 rewarded_nonserious_hack are positive, whereas
 baseline and attacker_legitimate_solve are
negative; the latter are challenging cases for detectors.
Ambiguous no_reward attempts are retained but excluded
from the binary population. 

 CheatBench. 

 CheatBench contains labeled agent trajectories with source-provided
 cheating , attempt , and benign outcomes
 ( CheatBench Maintainers 2026 ) . We collapse these labels and derive the
RH-Detect binary field by treating successful
 cheating traces as positive and benign traces as
negative; the 41 unsuccessful attempt traces are retained
but excluded from binary evaluation.

 A.3 Filtering, Expansion, and Splits 

 A stored paired row expands into two evaluation units; single
continuations yield one unit. When a source pairs continuations for
the same prompt, both units sharing the same exact prompt hash are assigned jointly
to the same split, and are resampled as a single cluster in
bootstrap procedures; sources without
prompt-level pairs retain native clean examples where available
(Jozdien and TRACE), use separately matched clean trajectories (MALT),
or use class-balanced clean samples from the same corpus
(Countdown-Code). Token length is computed per row from the concatenated message
contents using the cl100k_base encoding implemented in
the tiktoken tokenizer library; the primary
open-ended policy retains rows under 32,000 tokens, and per-experiment length policies
appear in Table S3 .
The prompt hash is computed from the canonical JSON prompt
serialization; the supervised experiments use two split protocols,
six source-held-out rotations for cross-source transfer and partitions within each track grouped by prompt-hash for cross-track
transfer, and no hash crosses a train/test boundary in either.

 B.2 Transcript Representations E1–E4b 

 Four transcript representations and one cross-extractor variant are
evaluated. E1 passes the raw role-agnostic transcript. E2 removes
detected reasoning spans while preserving the remaining transcript. E3
retains only the detected reasoning spans. E4 first extracts task
information and suspicion-relevant evidence verbatim and then applies
the unchanged detector prompt to that evidence document, using the same
model as extractor and judge. E4b keeps the Qwen Flash judge fixed but
uses GPT-5 Nano as the extractor. Table S5 gives the paired contrasts. 

 Reasoning-span rules (E2 and E3). 

 E2 retains externally visible behavior, whereas E3 retains reasoning
content. For non-agentic rows, reasoning is defined as text enclosed by
matched <think> or <thinking> tags, allowing
attributes and matched case-insensitively across line breaks; spans are
processed in document order, and an unclosed tag is ignored. The task
prompt is unchanged in both conditions: E2 removes all detected
reasoning spans from the continuation, while E3 keeps only those spans. Agentic trajectories usually lack explicit
reasoning tags, so the assignment is applied within each assistant
message: text before the submit call, or before the first tool
call when the message contains no submit call, is assigned to E3, and
the message content from that boundary onward is assigned to E2.
Assistant messages containing no tool or submit call are assigned
entirely to E3, and user and tool messages are always assigned to E2. Thus, agentic E2 contains the externally observable
interaction, whereas agentic E3 contains reasoning material only. The implementation is referenced in
Table S3 . 

 Extraction (E4 and E4b). 

 E4 uses self-extraction (Qwen Flash extracts for the Qwen Flash judge,
GPT-5 Nano for the GPT-5 Nano judge); E4b replaces only the extractor
for the Qwen Flash judge. The extractor runs at temperature 0 with a
4,096-token output limit under the contract summarized in Table S3 ; validation accepts an output only when
each required section appears exactly once and in order with non-empty
content. If any invalid or empty output triggers, the exact raw input text is used as the detector
input for that unit.

 D.2 Cross-Track Transfer 

 The cross-track experiment trains the same single-token objective
separately on each track under split grouped by prompt hash and
evaluates on both tracks with the fixed label orientation, i.e., YES marks the hacking continuation. Unlike the primary studies, the
open-ended split uses all 5,174 expanded units: long inputs are
middle-truncated to the 4,096-token rendered budget rather than
excluded, affecting 742 of 4,073 training, 171 of 587 validation, and
108 of 514 test units. The multiple-choice partitions contain 106,816
training, 13,344 validation, and 13,360 test units; every
multiple-choice input fits the same budget, so no multiple-choice unit required
truncation. One epoch takes 510 open-ended and 13,352
multiple-choice optimizer steps per seed, and because all six
open-ended sources appear in the open-ended training partition, the
open-ended test split does not measure source held-out transfer. The three-seed results appear in Table 7 .

 Appendix F Release and Reproducibility 

 Release and access. 

 The dataset and code will be released upon publication. The release
package includes the row-level data required for the
reported open-ended and multiple-choice experiments rather than the
entire unified benchmark. Specifically, it contains all 4,151
primary open-ended rows and all 66,760 multiple-choice rows.
Terminal Wrench, CheatBench, and the protected-response track are
not included at row level in the package. The accompanying ingestion
and integration code reconstructs the complete 92,761-row benchmark
from the original upstream sources. When the protected track is
reconstructed, its responses remain XOR-encrypted and prefixed with
the source canary. 

 Reproducibility. 

 The package includes the experiment-related data tables, the unified
schema, source ingestion and harmonization code, taxonomy-mapping
code, verbatim detector and training prompts, and the exact opaque
memberships for every reported experiment. It also provides
prompt-hash split validation, the API and vLLM evaluation harnesses,
and the final SFT and GRPO training scripts with fixed seeds. The
pinned environment file records the reported library versions
(TRL 1.7.0, Transformers 5.12.1, Torch 2.11.0, and vLLM 0.23.0). 

 The package contains no precomputed predictions, metrics, figures,
raw API responses, or checkpoints; these artifacts are generated
under a user-selected output directory when the workflows are
rerun. Reconstructing the complete benchmark may require access to
the original upstream sources, whereas rerunning the reported
experiments from the packaged tables requires the relevant API
credentials, model weights, and compute resources. Training used one
H100 GPU with 80 GB of memory, while a 40 GB MIG partition suffices
for the reported local evaluations. Under the provider pricing used
for the reported runs, evaluating one detector configuration cost
less than $5; current prices may differ.
